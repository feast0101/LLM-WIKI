# Active Recall

## Definition

Active recall is the practice of retrieving knowledge from memory without external cues. Instead of passively reading or reviewing material, active recall requires the learner to pull information from memory through testing, questioning, or free recall. This forces the brain to reconstruct knowledge rather than simply recognize it.

## Why It Matters

Active recall is one of the most powerful learning techniques because it strengthens memory traces and forces deeper encoding. The act of retrieval itself is a form of learning: each time you retrieve information, you change how it is stored, making it more accessible and flexible for future use. This contrasts sharply with passive study methods, which create fragile, easily-forgotten memories.

## Variants / Approaches

- **Self-testing**: Using flashcards, quizzes, or practice problems to test recall.
- **Free recall**: Writing down everything you remember about a topic without cues.
- **Elaborative interrogation**: Asking yourself "why" and "how" questions about material.
- **Teaching others**: Explaining material in your own words as a form of forced recall.

## Evidence

- Simply rereading or highlighting is often an illusion of competence; it feels good but doesn't stick well. [[sources/learning-how-to-learn]]
- Instead of rereading, read a section, look away, and recall the key ideas in your own words; use self-testing with flashcards and questions. [[sources/learning-how-to-learn]]
- Active recall forces the brain to retrieve and reconstruct knowledge, creating lasting memory and flexible understanding. [[sources/learning-how-to-learn]]
