# Myelin Sheath

## Definition

Myelin sheath is a fatty insulation wrapping around axons produced by Schwann cells in the peripheral nervous system and oligodendrocytes in the central nervous system. The wrapping is not continuous but rather segmented, with small gaps called nodes of Ranvier where the axon membrane is exposed.

## Why It Matters

Myelin dramatically affects the speed of neural signal transmission. The insulation causes action potentials to travel much faster along myelinated axons (~100 m/s) compared to unmyelinated axons (~1 m/s), a property that is exploited by the nervous system to prioritize signal types.

## Variants / Approaches

- **Myelinated axons**: Wrapped in myelin sheath; conduct action potentials rapidly via saltatory conduction (jumping from node to node).
- **Unmyelinated axons**: No myelin wrapping; conduct action potentials more slowly but continuously along the entire axon length.

## Evidence

- Action potentials travel at 1-100 m/s, with the range primarily determined by the presence or absence of myelin sheath insulation. [[sources/neuralink-and-the-brains-magical-future]]
- Myelinated axons conduct action potentials much faster than unmyelinated axons; this speed difference is exploited by the nervous system. [[sources/neuralink-and-the-brains-magical-future]]
- A practical example: when you stub your toe, sharp pain (carried by myelinated fibers) reaches the brain within ~1 second, while dull pain (carried by unmyelinated C fibers at ~1 m/s) takes 1-2 seconds longer. [[sources/neuralink-and-the-brains-magical-future]]
- Schwann cells wrap axons in layers of fatty myelin, similar to blankets. [[sources/neuralink-and-the-brains-magical-future]]
