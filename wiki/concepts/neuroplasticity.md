# Neuroplasticity

## Definition

Neuroplasticity is the ability of the brain's neural networks to structurally and functionally reorganize in response to experience, learning, and injury. It encompasses changes in synaptic strength (functional plasticity), the formation of new neural connections, and even migration or birth of neurons.

## Why It Matters

Neuroplasticity explains how learning occurs, how habits form and can be changed, and how the brain recovers from injury. It also means that each individual's brain is uniquely wired based on their particular experiences, which has profound implications for brain-machine interfaces—each brain's "neural code" is personalized.

## Variants / Approaches

- **Synaptic plasticity**: Changes in the strength of synaptic connections (long-term potentiation, long-term depression).
- **Structural plasticity**: Physical reorganization of neural circuits, including axon growth, synapse formation, and dendritic restructuring.
- **Functional plasticity**: Reorganization of functions to different brain regions after injury or learning.

## Evidence

- When you learn a new skill, initial improvements are due to chemical adjustments in neurotransmitter signaling; these changes are temporary. [[sources/neuralink-and-the-brains-magical-future]]
- With continued practice, the brain undergoes lasting structural changes that build hard-wired pathways encoding the skill; this is how learning becomes permanent. [[sources/neuralink-and-the-brains-magical-future]]
- Babies have the highest neuroplasticity, with their brains ready to adapt to any life path; neuroplasticity remains throughout life, allowing humans to learn and change habits. [[sources/neuralink-and-the-brains-magical-future]]
- Changing habits requires willpower to override existing neural pathways, but sustained effort causes the brain to physically rebuild those pathways, making the new behavior habitual. [[sources/neuralink-and-the-brains-magical-future]]
